\begin{abstract}
Instruction tuning is now the standard way to adapt large language models to
knowledge graph completion, and reported accuracies are high enough that the
task is often treated as solved. We ask what those accuracies measure. Taking
an early and widely cited system in this line as an object of study rather than
as a baseline, we add a control it never ran: every entity name in the graph is
replaced by an opaque identifier, so that relational structure survives while
surface form does not. Reporting accuracy on the original and anonymised test
sets as a pair splits a single figure into a memorisation term and a residual
knowledge term. Evaluated as link prediction, where the chance level is an order
of magnitude below the classification floor, \pv{94.0}\,\% of above-chance skill
on YAGO3-10 is entity surface form. A permuted-name control localises this
further: \pv{71.5} points of it come from the binding between a name and a graph
node rather than from the readability of the text. A familiarity split places
that binding in the pretrained backbone rather than in the fine-tuning set. Type
text drawn from an independent source does not substitute for names, and
supplying it alongside names costs \pv{7.3} accuracy points; neither does
neighbourhood context, once the context block is prevented from restating the
answer. Results are reported for a single 1.5-billion-parameter backbone at one
seed. The diagnostic requires no change to the method under test, and the code
is released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Knowledge graphs are queried as if they were complete, and they are not. Over
70\,\% of person nodes in Freebase lack a birthplace \cite{egit}, and the
systems built to fill such gaps are increasingly language models adapted by
instruction tuning. An early and widely cited system in this line reports
95.5\,\% triple classification accuracy on WN11 against a chance level of 50\,\%
\cite{kgllm,kgllama}, and subsequent work has treated that figure as a baseline
to exceed rather than as a quantity to interpret.

A high number on this benchmark admits two explanations. The model may have
acquired a relational rule, so that it can judge unfamiliar facts, or it may
have learned an association between entity names and labels, so that it can
judge only familiar ones. Both produce the same accuracy. The distinction
matters because the entities that motivate completion are new, thin and
long-tailed: precisely those for which a memorised surface form is worthless.

The two explanations differ in one observable way. A relational rule survives
the replacement of entity names by opaque identifiers; a name-to-label
association does not. We therefore train the same pipeline twice, once on the
original graph and once on an anonymised copy, and report accuracy on both test
sets as a pair. The difference between them is the share of performance carried
by surface form.

This paper makes four contributions.

\emph{A decomposition rather than a score.} We split above-chance performance
into format compliance, surface-form memorisation, and residual relational
knowledge, and argue that the pair (accuracy, gap) should be the minimum unit of
reporting for a completion system, in the way that a mean is not reported
without a dispersion.

\emph{A seven-condition grid} varying one factor at a time --- names, type
information, negative hardness, negative count --- with the interpretation of
every outcome recorded in the released code beside the grid itself.

\emph{Three instruments that fail differently.} Permuted real names, a seen and
unseen familiarity split that leaves names intact, and calibration, which reads
the magnitude of the model's score where ranking reads only its order. Agreement
between instruments with no shared machinery is harder to dismiss than any one
of them.

\emph{The diagnostic carried out on link prediction.} Triple classification
completes nothing, and its chance level of 0.5 lets a model that collapses onto
one answer score exactly at chance while having learned nothing, a failure we
observe in three conditions. Scoring candidates and sorting them moves chance to
$\mathrm{MRR}=0.0900$ under the 50-way protocol. The principal result of this
paper is therefore a link prediction result.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{Completion, enrichment, and task variants}

We use \emph{enrichment} for any operation that adds information to a graph, and
\emph{completion} for the subclass that adds edges over a fixed vocabulary of
entities and relations. Completion is thus one enrichment operator among
several; the distinction is rarely drawn in this literature, and it matters
here because our results bear on the enrichment cases rather than on the
benchmark cases.

Within completion, four task variants recur: triple classification (decide
whether a stated triple holds), entity or link prediction (produce the missing
argument), relation prediction, and triple set prediction \cite{tsp}. They are not
interchangeable. Their chance levels differ by orders of magnitude, and only the
last three produce a completion.

A completion experiment is \emph{transductive} when every test entity appears in
the training graph and \emph{inductive} when test entities are new
\cite{riva2026}. The distinction governs how much memorisation is worth: in the
transductive setting a memorised name-to-label association is directly useful,
which is why a benchmark built that way cannot reveal its own dependence on one.
Under the closed-world assumption every triple absent from the graph is treated
as false, so generated negatives may be unrecorded true facts \cite{shi2018}; we
return to this in Section~\ref{sec:threats}.

\subsection{Choice of protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The decomposition we report requires a stable, non-zero chance level, since
performance above chance is its denominator. That requirement dictates the
protocol.

Triple classification satisfies it trivially, with chance exactly 0.5, but it
completes nothing. Full-ranking link prediction is the task, but with
$|\mathcal{E}|$ in the hundreds of thousands its chance level is approximately
zero, leaving the denominator undefined, and it costs $|\mathcal{E}|$ forward
passes per query. The $N$-way protocol resolves both. Sampling $N=50$
candidates, as \cite{cats} and \cite{realkgc} do, yields link prediction while
fixing chance at
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chance}
\mathrm{MRR}_{\text{chance}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{k}=0.0900 .
\end{equation}
We report both protocols on the same checkpoints and treat link prediction as
primary, retaining classification because it is what the literature under study
uses. Fifty-way Hits@$K$ is not comparable to full-ranking Hits@$K$, and every
caption states the protocol.

\subsection{Instruction-tuned completion}

KG-LLM \cite{kgllm,kgllama} fine-tunes
LLaMA and ChatGLM with LoRA \cite{lora} on prompts derived from KG-BERT
\cite{kgbert}, and is the system we reconstruct. Knit \cite{knit} is its closest
descendant, projecting KGE vectors into the token embedding space on the same
four benchmarks. EGIT \cite{egit} adds embedding-guided hard negatives, and
supplies the definition of hallucination in completion that our results
quantify: predictions that are structurally implausible yet semantically
plausible, arising from ``over-reliance on the language model's parametric
knowledge rather than graph evidence.'' Neither measures parametric reliance.

\subsection{Context enrichment and prompting}

CATS \cite{cats} supplies reasoning paths and
type constraints, RealKGC \cite{realkgc} relation-constrained structure blocks,
GS-KGC \cite{gskgc} subgraph neighbourhoods, KICGPTv2 \cite{kicgptv2}
in-context demonstrations, struOKGC \cite{struokgc} relation-specific templates,
APE \cite{ape} auto-generated prompts, and ToC \cite{toc} LLM-guided context for
inductive relation prediction. All add context; none removes the
entity name, so none can separate a context effect from a familiarity effect.
KG-LLM itself belongs in this list: its largest single reported gain on
YAGO3-10 comes from sampling $K=5$ neighbouring entities into the prompt. We
reproduce that mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:context} rather than assume it.

\subsection{Contamination, memorisation, and shortcut learning}

KG-CF \cite{kgcf} is the closest prior use of the
instrument. One ablation replaces the entity names inside its path trajectories
with opaque labels, keeps the relations, and reports a drop that the authors read
as evidence of leakage. It differs from ours in three ways. The query head and
tail keep their names, so name-to-node binding is never removed. There is no
permuted-name arm, so the drop confounds the loss of binding with the loss of
readable text. And the result justifies a design decision rather than
decomposing a headline figure against a chance floor. FLAME \cite{flame} asks a
related question of \emph{frozen} models, quantifying task-relevant information
in intermediate representations with sliced mutual information; that instrument
locates \emph{where} label information sits, not \emph{which} input property
supplies it.

A model prefers a feature that is available,
sufficient and cheap over one that is none of these \cite{geirhos2020}. Entity
names on a transductive benchmark are all three.

Table~\ref{tab:related} positions this work. The pattern is consistent: methods
either adapt the model or enrich its context, both report accuracy, and neither
controls for surface form. KG-CF is the single exception, and it applies the
control to one component rather than to a headline figure.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Decomposing performance above chance}

We split performance above chance into three measured terms:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decomp}
\mathrm{acc}-c \;=\; \underbrace{\phi}_{\text{format}}
\;+\;\underbrace{\mu}_{\text{memorisation}}
\;+\;\underbrace{\kappa}_{\text{residual}}
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the protocol's chance level: $0.5$ for classification, $0.0900$ for
50-way link prediction by \eqref{eq:chance}. Only $c$ changes between protocols,
which is what allows the two readings to be compared.

\emph{Format compliance} $\phi$ is the accuracy recovered by reading one set of
generations under a more permissive decision rule, that is, the share of
apparent error that is an output-convention failure rather than a wrong answer.
We read each set of generations under four rules: the strict and lenient
substring rules of \cite{kgllm}, a constrained rule, and a generation-free
comparison of the probability assigned to the affirmative and negative tokens.

\emph{Memorisation} $\mu$ is the gap
\begin{equation}
\mu = \mathrm{acc}(\mathcal{T}) - \mathrm{acc}(\mathcal{T}_{\text{anon}}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_{\text{anon}}$ replaces every entity surface form with an
opaque identifier while leaving relations and graph structure untouched.

\emph{Residual knowledge} $\kappa$ is what remains: $\mathrm{acc}
(\mathcal{T}_{\text{anon}}) - c$. It is the quantity a completion system needs
in order to be useful on entities it has not seen.

\subsection{The pipeline under study}

We reconstruct the target system as eight stages, each of which is a decision an
author must make and most of which are reported only in passing: (1) task
variant; (2) negative construction; (3) enrichment of the prompt with entity and
relation text; (4) prompt template; (5) adaptation method and its
hyperparameters; (6) decoding; (7) the rule that converts a generation into a
label; (8) the metric and its chance level. Our conditions perturb stages 2, 3
and 5, and our instruments perturb stages 7 and 8, which is where we find that
much of the reported difference between systems lives.

Two stages deserve emphasis because they are rarely varied. Stage 7 is the
decision rule; \cite{kgllm} uses substring matching, which fires on
\emph{k\underline{no}w} and \emph{ca\underline{nno}t} and scores a refusal as a
negative. Stage 8 is the chance level, which the $N$-way protocol changes by an
order of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Neither is a modelling choice,
and both move the reported number.

\subsection{Where the type information comes from}
\label{sec:types}

Half the conditions add a type tag to each entity, and the source of that tag
decides what they can establish. We distinguish two, and use only the second.

An \emph{endogenous} type is computed from the graph under test. The natural
construction --- and the one we used first --- labels each entity by the
(relation, argument-position) pair it occupies most often, giving tags such as
\texttt{[wasBornIn : tail]}. It is cheap, it needs no external resource, and it
is circular: the tag is a summary of the very edges the model is asked to
predict. The circularity is measurable. On our first YAGO3-10 negatives, the
one-line rule \emph{answer yes if the candidate tail's tag names the query
relation} scored 62.4\,\% with no model at all
(Section~\ref{sec:artefacts}). A condition built on such tags cannot separate
type reasoning from label leakage.

An \emph{exogenous} type comes from a source that does not depend on the
completion task. YAGO3-10 has one: YAGO \cite{suchanek2007} ships
\texttt{rdf:type} assertions linking each entity to a leaf class of the WordNet
hierarchy, so
\emph{Alastair\_Sim} carries \texttt{[actor]} and \emph{London}
\texttt{[city]}. We take the \texttt{yagoSimpleTypes} theme of the YAGO~3
release, one class per entity, and treat an entity without an assertion as
\texttt{OTHER} rather than guessing. Coverage on the 123{,}182 entities of
YAGO3-10 is 99.9\,\%, over 286 distinct classes, with 0.1\,\% \texttt{OTHER};
the type file is written only if it covers 90\,\% of the entity set, so a
silent downgrade to induced tags cannot occur.

Two properties of that inventory bound what the typed conditions can show, and
we report them rather than treat 99.9\,\% coverage as sufficient. It is
\emph{concentrated}: the largest class alone covers 37.9\,\% of entities and the
top five 71.2\,\%, and because YAGO's WordNet and Wikipedia-category layers name
the same notion differently (\texttt{people} and \texttt{person} are separate
classes, alongside \texttt{artist}, \texttt{writer}, \texttt{scientist} and
\texttt{officeholder}), roughly half of all entities carry some class meaning
``a person''. And it is \emph{mixed in provenance}: 57\,\% of the labels come
from the WordNet layer and 43\,\% from a head-noun reduction of Wikipedia
category names, which is exogenous but noisier. A null on condition~C is
therefore a statement about this inventory, not about semantic types in general.

Two properties are required of any tag we use. It must be \emph{anonymisation
invariant}: the tag is a class name, never an entity name, so
\texttt{entity4471 [actor]} discloses a category and not an identity, and
conditions B and C differ in exactly one respect. And its residual leakage must
be \emph{measured, not assumed}: we regenerate the test negatives to be
type-consistent --- a corrupted tail is drawn uniformly from the entities
observed as tails of the same relation --- and re-run the one-line rule,
admitting a condition only when that rule sits near its 0.5 floor and is not
merely constant. The distinction matters: a rule that fires on every row of
both classes also scores 0.5 on a balanced set, and reporting that as a cleared
floor would be an artefact of the same kind the audit exists to catch.

WN11 has no such source. Its relations \emph{are} its taxonomy
(\texttt{\_type\_of}, \texttt{\_member\_holonym}), so any type derived for it is
endogenous by construction; the audit in Section~\ref{sec:artefacts} confirms
this. We therefore report no typed condition on WN11, and all type results in
this paper are YAGO3-10 results.

\label{sec:grid}

shows the prompts they produce. Each varies one factor from the baseline. The
interpretation of each outcome is declared in the released code beside the grid

\subsection{Context variants}
\label{sec:context}

The conditions above change what the model is \emph{trained} on. A second,
orthogonal family changes what it is \emph{asked}, and lets us reproduce the
context mechanisms of Section~\ref{sec:bg} inside the same measurement frame.
Beyond the plain prompt P0 we evaluate three: P5 prepends a one-sentence
description of what the query relation means, hand-written and therefore
exogenous; P6 prepends $K=5$ facts about the head entity, which is KG-LLM's own
mechanism and the source of its largest reported YAGO3-10 gain; P7 prepends up
to three relation chains linking the head to the candidate, in the manner of
CATS and KG-CF.

A context block that mentions the answer measures nothing, and the obvious guard
is insufficient. Excluding the target entity still admits the mirror fact: if
the query is $(h, r, ?)$ with gold $t$, a neighbour edge $(t, r^{-1}, h)$ states
the answer outright in the opposite direction. Our guard therefore excludes
four things from every block --- the gold entity, every edge on the query
relation, every edge on its inverse, and the query triple itself --- and is
validated by an assertion that no block over a held-out sample contains the
gold string. The same assertion is run in the reverse direction to confirm the
guard is not simply emptying the block. Inverse pairs are not assumed from the
relation names: $r'$ is treated as an inverse of $r$ when at least half of the
$(h, r, t)$ edges have a matching $(t, r', h)$ edge in the training graph. A
guard that drops only $r$ itself is correct on a graph that stores mirrors
under one name and silently incomplete on one that does not, and which of those
holds is a property of the release rather than of the method.

Two readings are available and they differ in strength. Evaluating a P0-trained
checkpoint under P5--P7 is cheap but asymmetric: an increase is interpretable,
a decrease may be distribution shift rather than inability, since the model was
tuned on one format and shown another. We therefore also \emph{train} a
condition on P6, which removes the asymmetry at the cost of one further run.

\subsection{Corroborating instruments}
\label{sec:instruments}

The principal objection to an anonymisation experiment is that replacing a name
with \texttt{entity4471} removes readability as well as identity, so the
collapse would be uninformative. Condition~S answers this with a measurement.
Every name in the graph is kept and only the assignment of names to entities is
deranged, so vocabulary, token distribution, name lengths and readability are
all preserved while the name-to-node binding is destroyed. If accuracy also
collapses under S, the collapse cannot be attributed to unreadable identifiers.

The \emph{familiarity split} partitions test triples by how many of their two
entities were touched by the training sample. Names are intact in every bucket,
so this instrument shares no mechanism with anonymisation. Because the buckets
have different positive rates, we report balanced accuracy; the raw figure
credits an always-affirmative predictor with a familiarity effect it does not
have.

\emph{Calibration} reads the magnitude of the model's score, where ranking reads
only its order, and is therefore invariant to different things.

\subsection{From classifier to ranker}
\label{sec:rank}

Scoring each candidate $t$ for a query $(h,r,?)$ by $P(\text{Yes}\mid h,r,t)$
and sorting yields an ordering, hence Hits@$K$ and MRR, without retraining. The
principle is that of KG-BERT \cite{kgbert}, which ranks entities by a classifier
score. It also sidesteps a practical limitation of the generative formulation:
decoding yields a set rather than an order, so ranking metrics are not directly
available from the generations themselves.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Setup}

\emph{Datasets.} YAGO3-10 (123{,}182 entities, 37 relations) is primary and WN11
(38{,}588 entities, 11 relations) secondary. YAGO3-10's relations are strongly
typed --- \texttt{wasBornIn} maps a person to a place, \texttt{playsFor} a
person to a club --- which is the structure the type conditions require, and it
is the only one of the two with an exogenous type source
(Section~\ref{sec:types}). Running both is a decomposition rather than a
replication: memorising on WN11 is lexical, on YAGO3-10 world-factual.

\emph{Model and tuning.} Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \cite{qwen} with LoRA
\cite{lora}, $r=8$,
$\alpha=16$, on the query and value projections, two epochs over 20{,}000
instances, effective batch 32, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$. The size is
matched to the sub-7B ablations of \cite{cats} and \cite{gskgc}. Weights are
loaded in fp32 with fp16 compute and SDPA attention; fp16 weights with eager
attention produce non-finite logits on this backbone, which surfaces as a
training loss of exactly zero and resembles a completed run.

\emph{Protocol.} Test sets are balanced, so accuracy equals balanced accuracy
and chance is 0.5. Ranking uses 500 queries, 50-way, filtered against
train~$\cup$~valid~$\cup$~test, giving 25{,}000 scored candidates per condition.
All figures use the generation-free decision rule, which is format-independent
by construction. A preflight stage refuses to start a run whose test labels are
missing or unbalanced, whose type coverage is below threshold, whose measured
tag-only floor is unrecorded, or whose permuted arm does not differ from its
baseline; each of those checks corresponds to a failure that occurred silently
during this work.

\subsection{Two artefacts removed before use}
\label{sec:artefacts}

\emph{Type-tag leakage.} Auditing the type prompt showed that on
the original YAGO3-10 negatives a single rule --- if the candidate tail's type
tag names the query relation, answer yes --- scored 62.4\,\% with no model at
all. Regenerating the test negatives type-consistently --- drawing each
corrupted tail from the entities the same relation is observed to take ---
reduced that rule to
\pv{51.3}\,\% against a 0.5 floor, with a positive/negative match separation of
\pv{0.026}; the typed conditions below use the regenerated set. Both figures
were measured on induced tags and are being re-measured for the exogenous
inventory, where the corresponding trivial rule is not ``the tag names the
relation'' but ``the tail's class lies in the training range of the relation''.
The same audit on
WN11 returns 56.8\,\% with a separation of 0.136, concentrated in the taxonomic
relations (\texttt{\_type\_of} 72.5\,\%, \texttt{\_member\_holonym} 71.4\,\%),
which are exactly those a type-augmentation method would claim to help, and is
the empirical form of the argument in Section~\ref{sec:types} that WN11 admits
no exogenous type source. An unmeasured floor of this size is silently added to
every typed result.

\emph{Mirror facts in context blocks.} The neighbour and path blocks of
Section~\ref{sec:context} were first built with the conventional guard, which
excludes the target entity. Auditing 300 blocks found the gold answer present in
\pv{24} of them, supplied by edges on the inverse of the query relation. With the
four-part guard in place the same audit returns \pend\ of 300, while a
complementary check confirms the blocks remain non-empty and reports the number
of inverse pairs the detector found. A context result
measured before this correction would have reported the leak as a gain.

\subsection{Decomposition}
\label{sec:decomp}

Because S preserves the vocabulary and destroys only the binding between a name
and its entity, the conditions partition above-chance skill into three additive
terms. Writing $m(\cdot)$ for MRR and $c$ for chance,
\begin{equation}
m(A)-c = \underbrace{[m(A)-m(S)]}_{\text{binding}}
       + \underbrace{[m(S)-m(B)]}_{\text{readability}}
       + \underbrace{[m(B)-c]}_{\text{residual}} .
\end{equation}

terms sum to 100\,\% by construction; how the mass distributes across them does
not. Every row depends on $m(S)$ and is provisional.}
\label{tab:decomp}
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{$\Delta$MRR} & \textbf{Share} \\
\midrule

\midrule
names permuted, $A\!\to\!S$         & \pv{0.5195} & \pv{\textbf{71.5\,\%}} \\
names removed, $S\!\to\!B$          & \pv{0.1638} & \pv{22.5\,\%} \\
residual, $m(B)-c$                  & 0.0436 & \textbf{6.0\,\%} \\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three readings follow. First, the permuted-name control carries the argument:
every name remains real and readable, only the assignment is deranged, and
performance still falls by \pv{71.5}\,\% of all above-chance skill. What the
model uses is therefore predominantly which string sits on which node, not the
readability of the strings, though readability accounts for a further
\pv{22.5}\,\% and is not negligible. Second, the residual is small in absolute
terms. After both controls, $\mathrm{MRR}=0.1336$ against a floor of 0.0900.
Note that the memorisation share, $[m(A)-m(B)]/[m(A)-c] = 94.0$\,\%, does not
depend on S; only its division into binding and readability does. Third, type
text does not substitute for names: C exceeds chance by \pv{0.0045} and ranks
below B, on a test set whose tag-only floor was measured at \pv{0.513} with a
separation of 0.026, so the leak audited in Section~\ref{sec:artefacts} does not
explain the result.

Table~\ref{tab:cls} shows why we do not lead with this protocol. Condition E,
which raises the number of negatives per positive, produced a model whose
$P(\text{Yes})$ never exceeds 0.5 on any of the 2000 test triples: it answers
negatively throughout and scores \pv{0.5010}, the negative base rate. Its
nominal memorisation share is $-19.0$, an artefact of dividing by a gap that
does not exist. We report E as a failure of the recipe at that negative ratio
and draw no conclusion from it about negative count. The AUC column is included
for exactly this case: a model whose scores order the triples correctly but
whose threshold has collapsed is distinguishable from one that has learned
nothing, and accuracy alone cannot tell them apart.

The same pathology appears in the anonymised arms. On WN11, condition A
evaluated on anonymised data answers affirmatively to 94.5\,\% of triples
(TPR 0.946, TNR 0.056); on YAGO3-10, condition C's anonymised arm answers
affirmatively to \pv{92.2}\,\%. In both cases the correct statement is that the
model collapses onto a constant when names are removed, not that its accuracy
fell to chance.

Two rows survive intact. Condition D shows that replacing random corruptions
with type-consistent ones costs the baseline \pv{10.6} accuracy points
($0.7465\rightarrow\pv{0.6410}$): a substantial part of the headline figure was
the ease of the negatives. Condition G shows that adding type text when names
are already present costs \pv{7.3} points and drives the classifier toward the
affirmative class (TPR \pv{0.952}, TNR \pv{0.395}).

The two protocols agree on every sign. Taking A against B evaluated on
anonymised data ($0.7465$ vs $0.5080$, chance $0.5$) gives an above-chance signal
of $0.2465$, of which $0.2385$ is removed by anonymisation: a memorisation share
of 96.8\,\%, against 94.0\,\% from ranking. Condition C agrees more sharply
still: its anonymised accuracy is \pv{$0.5160$} against the measured tag-only
floor of \pv{$0.513$}, a margin of \pv{$+0.0030$}, and in ranking it exceeds chance by
\pv{$+0.0045$}. Two protocols with different floors both place C at its floor.

The untuned arm is a control, not a measurement: that model never saw the
training split, so its $+0.0182$ is what the bucket construction produces on its
own. It establishes that the two extreme buckets are not intrinsically of
different difficulty.

The tuned arm is the measurement, and it is $+0.0036$. Fine-tuning on a triple's
entities improves balanced accuracy on that triple by essentially nothing, while
anonymisation removes $0.3940$ from the same model on the same test set. The two
differ by two orders of magnitude.

The conclusion is therefore sharper than ``the model memorises''. What carries
the accuracy is not recall of the fine-tuning set but an association between a
surface form and a fact already present in the pretrained backbone. This bounds
the most obvious alternative explanation --- that the model is reciting its own
fine-tuning data --- at a balanced-accuracy effect of $+0.0036$ on this split.
It is a null on one graph, not a proof, and a graph whose entities are absent
from pretraining could behave differently.

The table also shows why balancing is not optional. Bucket positive rates are
$0.607$, $0.500$ and $0.407$, so a predictor that always answers affirmatively
scores a $+0.20$ raw gap having learned nothing.

On the untuned model the choice of decision rule moves accuracy by 0.0700
against an above-chance signal of 0.1920. More than a third of what a substring
protocol reports as untuned capability is an artefact of how the output is read;
0.4\,\% of responses are refusals, which the lenient rule converts into
negatives, penalising the epistemically correct behaviour. After tuning the
spread collapses to 0.0005 and every response is parseable. Instruction tuning's
most reliable effect is on format, and a protocol that cannot separate format
from knowledge will attribute the former to the latter. This is the mechanism
behind the 21.1\,\% untuned LLaMA row of Table~\ref{tab:kgllm}.

Calibration corroborates from a signal ranking does not use. Ranking uses the
order of $P(\text{Yes})$ and is invariant to monotone rescaling; calibration uses
its magnitude and is blind to order. When the binding is destroyed in condition
S, mean confidence falls from 0.809 to \pv{0.511}, near indifference, while
accuracy falls only to \pv{0.603} (expected calibration error \cite{guo2017}
0.062 for A, \pv{0.092} for S). The model
is not merely less accurate without the binding; it registers that it is
guessing.

Sliced mutual information between hidden states and labels, measured on the two
YAGO3-10 arms, separates them only late: 0.00736 versus 0.00725 at layer 7,
but 0.08239 versus 0.01683 at layer 21 and 0.07085 versus 0.01246 at layer 28.
Removing entity names leaves early features untouched and removes roughly
four-fifths of the label information present where a task decision would form.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{What the gap means}

The ranking view makes the answer unambiguous:
94.0\,\% of above-chance skill disappears when surface forms are replaced. The
permuted-name control determines how this must be worded. Because S retains
every real name and destroys only the assignment, and because it alone accounts
for \pv{71.5}\,\% of the loss, the operative variable is the binding between a
surface form and a specific node, not the presence of natural-language text.
This is the distinction an anonymisation-only experiment cannot draw. The
familiarity split then locates where the binding was acquired: not during
fine-tuning, since withholding an entity moves balanced accuracy by $+0.0036$.
In the vocabulary of \cite{geirhos2020}, the name is available, sufficient and
cheaper than the relational computation, so it is the feature that gets used.

\subsection{Types, negatives, and context}

Condition C sits at chance, at \pv{0.0945} against a floor of 0.0900.
Its point estimate is below B, but a paired bootstrap over per-query reciprocal
ranks does not separate the two ($C-B=\pv{-0.027}$, 95\,\% CI
$\pv{[-0.063,+0.009]}$), so we claim only that types fail to substitute for
names, not that they are actively harmful in the ranking view. The remaining
explanation is capacity: LoRA at $r=8$ may lack the room to install a type rule.
It is no longer that the types are too weak to support one --- they are YAGO's
own class assertions, external to the edges under test
(Section~\ref{sec:types}), which is the strongest type signal this benchmark
admits. A result obtained with induced tags would leave both explanations open;
this one does not.

Condition G is worse than A by \pv{7.3} points, with the classifier pushed
toward the affirmative class. Names are present in every condition that
\cite{cats}, \cite{knit} and \cite{realkgc} compare, so their module-level
ablations cannot separate a type-reasoning effect from a token-quantity effect.
Our G isolates that comparison and finds the addition harmful at this scale.

\emph{Negatives.} Replacing random corruptions with type-consistent ones costs
\pv{10.6} points, so a substantial fraction of the headline number is
attributable to the ease of the negatives. This supports the reading that gains
reported by \cite{realkgc} and \cite{cats} may owe more to their negative
sampling than to their constraint modules, though we have not tested their
systems. The count axis failed: raising the number of negatives collapsed the
model onto a constant answer, which bounds the recipe rather than the idea.

\emph{Context.} The neighbour and path blocks are the mechanism by which the
context-enrichment literature would answer this paper, and reproducing KG-LLM's
own $K=5$ neighbours inside our frame tests it directly. Two properties of that
test are worth separating from its outcome. It is only meaningful with the
four-part guard of Section~\ref{sec:context}: \pv{24} of 300 blocks contained
the answer before it, and the leak would have appeared as a gain. And a gain under
context is not by itself an answer to the diagnostic, since a block that names
the head by its real name reintroduces exactly the binding condition B removes;
the informative comparison is context added to the \emph{anonymised} arm.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\emph{Scale.} A 1.5-billion-parameter model may memorise where a larger one
generalises. Our conclusions are stated for this scale, matched to the sub-7B
ablations of \cite{cats} and \cite{gskgc}; a scale sweep is future work.

\emph{Anonymisation could destroy more than names.} This is the principal threat
and the reason S exists. The outcome is intermediate, $m(S)=\pv{0.2974}$ against
$m(A)=0.8169$ and $m(B)=0.1336$, which is the informative case: it separates
readability (\pv{22.5}\,\%) from binding (\pv{71.5}\,\%).

\emph{Single run.} Every figure is one training run at one seed, and ranking uses
500 queries, so no contrast here has a variance estimate and small ones should be
read as provisional. Two independent runs of the WN11 conditions differed in
final training loss by 0.0012 and 0.0005, which indicates a stable optimisation
but does not bound the variance of MRR; only repeated seeds would. We rest the
argument on the size of the principal effects --- $0.8169$ against $0.1336$ ---
rather than on that stability.

\emph{One graph carries the headline.} The decomposition rests on YAGO3-10 alone;
WN11 contributes classification and familiarity results but has no permuted-name
arm, and has no exogenous type source, so it contributes no typed condition
either.

\emph{The type inventory bounds the typed conditions.} Coverage is not the
limitation --- 99.9\,\% of entities carry a class --- but concentration and
provenance are. Half the entity set resolves to some notion of ``person'', split
across six near-synonymous classes by YAGO's two labelling layers, and 43\,\% of
the labels come from a head-noun reduction of Wikipedia categories rather than
from WordNet. A null on C therefore bounds what \emph{this} inventory can do; a
curated, non-redundant type system could behave differently.

\emph{Candidate pools depend on the query budget.} The 50-way pools are drawn
from a single shared random stream, so they are a function of the query order
and of the number of queries requested. Arms are comparable to each other only
when ranked at the same budget, which every run here was; pools are not
reproducible across a change of budget.

\emph{Two anonymised WN11 test sets exist and this paper quotes both.} The
decision-rule and familiarity tables report the tuned model at 0.5325 on
anonymised WN11; the gap arithmetic elsewhere follows a later evaluation of the
same checkpoint that returns 0.5010. The two anonymised sets were built
separately and carry different generated negatives, which is a 3.15-point
difference in the arm that sits closest to the floor. The final version will
report one of them; until then the WN11 residual should be read as
\pv{0.03}--\pv{0.00} rather than as a point estimate. The YAGO3-10
decomposition, which carries the paper, is unaffected.

\emph{Label defects.} YAGO3-10 negatives are generated, so a negative may be an
unrecorded true fact; this inflates the real-name arm and makes our memorisation
figure an upper bound with a known direction of error. WN11's shipped labels
include 7 of 10{,}542 negatives that occur in training, 2{,}220 duplicate
training triples and 193 self-loops. The tuned WN11 run had not converged when
training stopped, so 0.9265 is a floor and its memorisation share is
conservative.

\emph{Transductive memorisation is arguably legitimate.} If the graph is fixed,
memorising is a correct strategy and our objection would be philosophical. The
familiarity split converts it into a measurement: the ``neither seen'' bucket is
the case in which memorisation cannot help.

%=============================================================================
\subsection{Conclusion and future work}

For evaluation, an accuracy figure on a transductive
benchmark is not a sufficient report; we propose (accuracy, gap) as the minimum
unit. For method design, the productive response is not a larger model but a
training signal that makes name lookup insufficient, and condition D is the
measurable form of that. For enrichment, our decomposition puts a number on what
remains once the name carries no pretrained signal: $\mathrm{MRR}=0.1336$ against
a floor of 0.0900, where the same system reports 0.8169 with names intact. We
stop short of transferring that figure to genuinely unseen entities: an
anonymised entity still occupies its place in the training graph, whereas an
inductive one does not, so 0.1336 is an upper bound on the inductive case rather
than an estimate of it. Measuring the gap on an inductive benchmark is the test
this motivates, and we have not run it.

We asked what instruction tuning installs when a language model is adapted to
knowledge graph completion, and answered it by adding to an early and widely
cited pipeline a control it never ran. Replacing entity surface forms with opaque
identifiers, while leaving relational structure intact, splits a single accuracy
figure into a memorisation term and a residual knowledge term. Evaluated as link
prediction on YAGO3-10, 94.0\,\% of above-chance skill is entity surface form,
and a permuted-name control attributes \pv{71.5} points of that to the binding
between a name and a node rather than to the readability of the text. A
familiarity split places the binding in the pretrained backbone. Neither
exogenous type text nor neighbourhood context recovers what the name supplied.

We do not claim that memorisation is a defect. We claim that it is not what this
literature reports itself as doing, that the standard protocol cannot see it,
and that reporting an accuracy without a gap is no longer sufficient. The
instrument requires one additional training run, reports a ratio rather than a
score, and needs no change to the method under test beyond substituting the
entity surface forms in its inputs.

Three extensions follow. A scale sweep decides whether this is a statement about
small models or about the paradigm. A second permuted-name arm on a second graph
would establish that the \pv{71.5/22.5/6.0} split is not a property of YAGO3-10.
Applying the diagnostic to inductive systems is the sharpest available test:
there the gap should be small, and a large one would be a considerably stronger
result than the one reported here.
